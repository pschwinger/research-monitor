Rett Syndrome — where treatments & research stand

A calm, honest snapshot of real progress toward treating Rett syndrome, with a focus on the MECP2 R255X variant. Refreshed every three months so the people who care can follow the science without chasing it.

Overall grounding 89%
Rett syndrome is caused by changes in a single gene, MECP2. For a long time there was nothing to offer but supportive care. That has genuinely changed: there is now an approved medicine for the symptoms, and — for the first time — therapies aimed at the root cause are in human trials with encouraging results. Available now

Trofinetide (Daybue) — treating the symptoms

First medicine approved for Rett syndrome
Grounding 95%

Trofinetide is the first medicine ever approved specifically for Rett syndrome. It doesn't fix the gene, but in trials it improved communication and behavioural symptoms. It's approved in the US and Canada (as Daybue).

Europe — a real turning point: after an initial rejection in February 2026, European regulators (the EMA's CHMP) reversed course and recommended approval on 26 June 2026. A final European Commission decision is expected in the coming months — which would make it the first approved Rett therapy in the EU.

In human trials

Gene therapy — going after the root cause

One-time treatment, delivers a working copy of MECP2
Grounding 90%

This is the biggest source of hope. Instead of easing symptoms, gene therapy aims to give cells a functional copy of the MECP2 gene — addressing the actual cause. Two programs are furthest along:

  • NGN-401 (Neurogene) — updated Phase 1/2 results (June 2026) are encouraging: participants gained developmental milestones (about 4.7 new milestones each) with continued improvement out to 30 months, and it has been generally well tolerated across 35 participants with no treatment-related serious safety events at the studied dose. Its registrational trial has finished dosing; fuller "topline" results are expected in the second half of 2027.
  • TSHA-102 (Taysha) — a one-time therapy delivered into the spinal fluid. The pivotal (Phase 3) trial began dosing in late 2025, and the company has aligned with the FDA on the path toward a filing for approval.

These are still investigational — not yet approved — but they are in the decisive stage of testing.

Earlier-stage research

Specific to R255X — repairing the “stop” signal

Read-through & engineered-tRNA approaches (lab stage)
Grounding 80%

The R255X change is a "nonsense" mutation — it inserts a premature stop signal so the protein is cut short. It's one of the most common Rett mutations (~35% of cases are this type), which makes it a major research target. A promising idea is to make the cell "read through" that false stop and build the full protein again:

  • Read-through compounds — newer molecules (e.g. CDX enhancers with low-dose gentamicin) restored full-length protein in cells, aiming to avoid the toxicity of older drugs.
  • Engineered tRNAs — custom-designed molecules that overrode the false stop and restored up to ~60% of the full protein in laboratory tests.

These results are in cells and lab models — not yet in people — but they're aimed squarely at R255X.
