Rett Syndrome — the full picture for families

An honest, plain-language deep-dive into everything that matters if you love someone with Rett syndrome (with a focus on the MECP2 R255X variant): approved medicines, every avenue in trials, the genetic "cure" research, day-to-day care, and how to reach trials. Refreshed every three months.

Rett syndrome comes from changes in one gene, MECP2. For decades there was only supportive care. That has genuinely changed: there is now an approved medicine, more drugs in trials, and — for the first time — therapies aimed at the root cause are in humans. 💊 Medicines
Available now

Trofinetide (Daybue)

First medicine approved for Rett syndrome · treats symptoms
Grounding 95%

The first medicine ever approved specifically for Rett syndrome. It eases communication and behavioural symptoms (it doesn't fix the gene). Approved in the US & Canada. In Europe, after an initial rejection, regulators reversed course and recommended approval on 26 June 2026 — a final EU decision is expected within months, which would make it the first approved Rett therapy in the EU.

In trials

Blarcamesine (ANAVEX 2-73) & Fenfluramine (Fintepla)

Other drugs being tested for symptoms & seizures
Grounding 85%
  • Blarcamesine (ANAVEX 2-73) — an oral drug acting on the sigma-1 receptor. Results are mixed: an adult Phase 3 (AVATAR) showed significant benefit, but the pediatric Phase 2/3 (EXCELLENCE, 92 children) improved behaviour without clearly beating placebo. Still under evaluation.
  • Fenfluramine (Fintepla) — already used for other epilepsies; the maker (UCB) planned to start a Phase 3 program in Rett in 2026, aimed mainly at seizures.
🧬 Gene therapy — going after the root cause
In human trials

NGN-401 & TSHA-102

One-time therapies that deliver a working MECP2 gene
Grounding 90%

The biggest source of hope: instead of easing symptoms, these give cells a functional copy of MECP2, addressing the actual cause. Two programs lead:

  • NGN-401 (Neurogene) — updated Phase 1/2 data (June 2026) is encouraging: children gained developmental milestones (~4.7 each), improving out to 30 months, and it was generally well tolerated across 35 participants with no treatment-related serious safety events at the studied dose. Its registrational trial has finished dosing; fuller results are expected 2nd half of 2027.
  • TSHA-102 (Taysha) — a one-time therapy delivered into the spinal fluid. Its pivotal (Phase 3) trial began dosing in late 2025, with an agreed path toward a filing for approval.

Investigational — not yet approved — but in the decisive testing stage.

🔧 Repairing the gene itself
Lab research

Reawakening the healthy “silent” copy (X-reactivation)

Could work for any mutation, including R255X
Grounding 78%

Girls with Rett usually carry one healthy MECP2 copy that sits switched off on the inactive X chromosome. A major research push aims to switch that healthy copy back on — which would help regardless of the specific mutation. In the lab, combining an antisense molecule (targeting "Xist") with drugs that loosen DNA silencing produced strong reactivation, and epigenome editing restored MECP2 in patient-derived neurons. Still preclinical, but conceptually powerful.

Lab research

R255X-specific: reading through the “stop” signal

Read-through drugs & engineered tRNAs
Grounding 80%

R255X is a "nonsense" mutation — it inserts a premature stop signal that cuts the protein short. It's one of the most common Rett mutations (~35% of cases), making it a big research target. The idea is to make the cell ignore that false stop and build the whole protein again: newer read-through compounds (e.g. CDX enhancers with low-dose gentamicin) and engineered tRNAs restored up to ~60% of the full protein in lab tests. In cells for now, but aimed squarely at R255X.

🩺 Managing symptoms & health (day to day)
Standard care

What to watch and treat

From the IRSF Comprehensive Care Guidelines
Grounding 92%
  • Seizures (~74% of people) — managed with anti-seizure medicines; a ketogenic diet can help hard-to-control seizures (under specialist supervision).
  • Heart rhythm (QT prolongation) — an important, often-missed risk. Regular EKGs are recommended, and some medicines that prolong the QT interval should be used with caution.
  • Breathing irregularities — breath-holding/hyperventilation; screen for sleep apnea with a sleep study if snoring or pauses occur.
  • Digestion — constipation (~65%) and reflux are common and treatable; some children need a feeding tube for growth/safety.
  • Spine (scoliosis) — common, usually appearing ages 8–11; monitored regularly, occasionally needs surgery.
  • Bones — higher fracture risk; attention to vitamin D, calcium and weight-bearing activity.

Care is best coordinated by a multidisciplinary team — this is general information, not a treatment plan.

🤝 Everyday therapies that help quality of life
Supportive

Communication, movement, music & diet

What families and therapists find helps most
Grounding 86%
  • Eye-gaze communication — eye-tracking devices (AAC) are one of the most meaningful tools, letting many girls communicate choices and connect, despite limited hand use.
  • Physical therapy — maintains mobility and prevents joint tightening (contractures); occupational therapy supports hand use and daily skills.
  • Music therapy — encouraging evidence: better communication, eye contact, social engagement and hand use — and in one study, fewer seizures.
  • Hydrotherapy & movement — swimming/treadmill/sensory approaches help function and enjoyment.
  • Diet & supplements — ketogenic diet for seizures (supervised); CBD/CBDV shows anti-seizure promise in early studies. Discuss any supplement with the care team.
📋 Getting into trials & staying informed
Actionable

Registries, natural-history & expert centers

The practical route to trials and best care
Grounding 90%
  • Join a registry — the RSRT Global Registry and the IRSF Rett Syndrome Registry are the main ways families get notified about eligible trials and contribute data that speeds research.
  • Natural History Study — a 15-year US study of 1,000+ individuals underpins today's trials and care standards.
  • Centers of Excellence — specialist Rett clinics (many in the US, e.g. UNC, CHOP) run trials and coordinate complex care; Rett Syndrome Europe runs a European registry and connects families across countries.
  • ClinicalTrials.gov — the official, always-current list of every active Rett trial worldwide.
